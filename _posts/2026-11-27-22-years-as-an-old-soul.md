I was the kid adults called an “old soul,” the student whose report cards came home with some version of "a joy to have in class," the one who would rather join in on the grown-ups’ conversation than run off to play. For as long as I can remember, I've felt older than the numerical age I was given.

Now I'm about to turn 22, and the feeling hasn't faded. I've been called “mature for my age” my whole life, and while it was always meant as a compliment, I've wondered where that maturity came from and whether other adoptees have felt it too.

Even when I was little, I felt out of place with the kids around me, as if I was viewing the world from a couple of years ahead. Part of that comes from feeling different from the very beginning. I was adopted from Hunan, China, and raised as an only child in a family where no one looked like me. Although no one in my family ever tried to make me feel different for being Chinese, there were times in school when I did.

When I was in first grade, a kid in my class said, “I can’t understand you because you’re Chinese.” I was speaking English like everyone else, so my first-grade mind was just confused about what being Chinese had to do with anything. I did know that this kid was judging me for something, and the only part of the judgment I understood was “Chinese.” It didn’t dawn on me that this could be xenophobia, and First Grade Me wouldn’t have had the vocabulary to articulate it. That day was a sobering moment for my parents, who, as white people, had never faced a comment like that, and then they had to explain the significance of that kid’s words to me. That's a complex thing for a six-year-old to face, and I think moments like that pushed me to grow up a little faster.

That said, personality, temperament, and the way my parents raised me all played their part. Being an only child, I spent my time around adults and absorbed their conversations and mannerisms, so adult behavior became my normal, and “kid behavior” seemed frivolous to me. Still, I do believe my adoption contributed. When your life begins with a loss you can't remember and don’t fully understand, you learn early that things aren't always simple, fair, or easily explainable. You start asking bigger questions sooner. 

For someone who feels so old on the inside, I'm a very small Asian girl, and the world tends to treat me accordingly. It's a strange thing to feel like the oldest person in the room while being seen as the youngest. And then there are the smaller, sillier signs. I listen to music from the 1970s and have since middle school, which means that Middle-schooler Me somehow became the kid in the car singing every word to songs released decades before she was born. It's a running joke in my family.

For a long time, I assumed that once I reached some particular birthday, my mind would finally snap into step with everyone else's, and I would be the same age on the inside as my peers. So far, that birthday hasn’t come, and I've stopped waiting for it. If anything, being an old soul has given me the ability to think deeply, to connect with people, and to stand on my own two feet. I wouldn't trade it to feel my age, because it's one of the many ways my story has shaped me into who I am.
